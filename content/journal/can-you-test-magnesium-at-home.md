**Short answer:** you can order a finger-prick magnesium test online, but no blood test, home or clinic, gives a clear picture of your body's magnesium. Less than 1% of it is in your blood [1][2]. The body holds blood levels in a narrow range by drawing on bone and other tissue, so a normal result doesn't rule out low stores [2][3]. For most people, the more useful check is your diet: about 44% of US women and 49% of US men get less than the average requirement, even counting supplements [4].

## Where your magnesium actually is

An adult body holds about 25 g of magnesium. Most of it sits in bone and inside cells [1].

| Where | Share of body magnesium |
|---|---|
| Bone | About 53% [2] |
| Muscle | About 27% [2] |
| Other soft tissue | About 19% [2] |
| Red blood cells | About 0.5% [2] |
| Serum (the liquid part of blood) | About 0.3% [2] |

Up to a third of the magnesium in bone can be swapped in and out. When blood levels start to drop, this larger pool tops them up. The authors of a 2018 review call this "a key reason why blood measurements can easily mask deficiency" [2].

## The standard test: serum magnesium

This is the test doctors use most [5]. It measures magnesium in serum. The common reference range is **0.75–0.95 mmol/L** (about 1.8–2.3 mg/dL) [3].

What it's good for: spotting a clearly low or high level, which matters for people with kidney disease, heavy diarrhea or certain medicines [1].

What it can't do:

- **Show your stores.** Serum levels have little link with total body magnesium [1][2].
- **Catch mild shortfalls reliably.** That range was set in the 1970s from the spread of values in people assumed to be healthy, not from health outcomes [3]. Some magnesium researchers argue the lower cut-off should be raised to 0.85 mmol/L [3][5]. That is an expert opinion, not an agreed standard, but it shows how much a "normal" result depends on where the line is drawn.

Ranges also differ between labs, so read your result against the range printed on your own report [2].

## Other tests, and their limits

| Test | What it measures | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **RBC magnesium** (red blood cells) | Magnesium inside red cells, which hold more than serum | Often marketed as "better", but few studies have tested it properly against diet changes over months [2] |
| **Ionized magnesium** | The active, unbound form in blood | May reflect status better, but its use is still debated [3] |
| **24-hour urine** | How much you pass in a day | Varies a lot from day to day in the same person (36% in one study) [2] |
| **Magnesium loading test** | How much of an IV dose your body keeps | Research tool only: costly and impractical for most clinics [2] |
| **Saliva** | Magnesium in saliva | Not well matched to other measures [2] |
| **Hair analysis** | Minerals in a hair sample | Unreliable: 6 US labs given the same hair sample gave results more than 10-fold apart for 12 minerals [6] |

A 2011 systematic review of supplementation and depletion trials found that serum, RBC and urine magnesium all moved when people changed their intake. That makes them useful for comparing groups of people. It doesn't make any single result a precise read on one person's stores [7].

One thing to know: two of the three authors of the 2018 review work for Balchem, a nutrition ingredient company, and the third was a paid consultant to it [2]. The limits they describe match the independent sources here [1][3][7].

## What home kits measure

Home kits mail a few drops of finger-prick blood to a lab. Depending on the kit, the report shows serum (or "blood") magnesium or RBC magnesium. Before you buy:

- **Check the test name.** "Magnesium" on its own usually means serum.
- **Expect the same limits as above.** A home sample doesn't get around the fact that blood holds so little of your magnesium.
- **Ask how the lab checks its method** against standard blood testing, and what range it uses.
- **Skip hair tests** for magnesium [6].

A home result can be a starting point for a conversation with your doctor. It's not a reason to start or stop a supplement on its own.

## Who is most likely to fall short

National survey data (NHANES 2015–2018) show how common low intake is. These figures include supplements:

| Group | Below the average requirement |
|---|---|
| Women 19–30 | 50% [4] |
| Women 31–50 | 43% [4] |
| Women 51–70 | 41% [4] |
| Men 19–30 | 51% [4] |
| Men 71+ | 56% [4] |
| All adults | 44% (women), 49% (men) [4] |

Low intake isn't the same as deficiency. The kidneys hold on to magnesium when intake is low, so clear deficiency is uncommon in otherwise healthy people [1]. The groups more likely to run low, according to NIH, are [1]:

- **Older adults.**
- **People with type 2 diabetes**, who lose more in urine.
- **People with digestive conditions** such as Crohn's disease or celiac disease.
- **People with alcohol dependence.**
- **People taking some medicines** long term, such as acid reducers (PPIs) or loop and thiazide diuretics.

## Symptoms are a poor guide

Early signs of low magnesium include loss of appetite, nausea, tiredness and weakness. More serious shortfalls can bring numbness, tingling, muscle cramps and abnormal heart rhythms [1]. The problem is that these are common and have many causes. Low magnesium "does not present with unique and identifiable clinical manifestations" [2]. Tiredness or the odd cramp is not a sign that you need magnesium.

## A practical check: your diet

You can estimate your intake for free. Adults need **310–320 mg a day (women)** and **400–420 mg (men)** [1]. Good sources include pumpkin seeds (156 mg per ounce), chia seeds (111 mg per ounce), almonds (80 mg per ounce), boiled spinach (78 mg per half cup) and black beans (60 mg per half cup) [1]. Log a few typical days in a free nutrition app and compare.

If you fall short and add a supplement, keep supplements at or below **350 mg a day**, unless your doctor advises more. Food doesn't count toward that limit [1].

## When to see a doctor

- You have **muscle cramps with tingling or numbness**, or a **racing or irregular heartbeat**.
- You have **kidney disease**. Excess magnesium can build up, so don't take supplements unless your doctor prescribes them [1].
- You take a **PPI** or a **diuretic** long term. Your doctor may check your levels [1].
- You have **type 2 diabetes**, a **digestive condition**, or drink heavily [1].
