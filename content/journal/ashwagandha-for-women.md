**Short answer:** the main ashwagandha trials on stress and sleep included women, and their results apply to women as much as men: lower stress scores and cortisol, and modestly better sleep, after about 8 weeks [1][2][3]. A few small trials looked only at women, on menopause symptom scores and sexual function. They were positive but small, short and often supplied by the extract's maker [4][5][6][7]. Ashwagandha can also raise thyroid hormones, has been linked to rare liver injury, and **should be avoided in pregnancy and while breastfeeding** [8][9][10][11].

## Stress and cortisol: women were in the trials

Most ashwagandha trials enrolled both men and women and reported the results together:

- **KSM-66, 60 days:** 64 adults with chronic stress, 23 of them women. 600 mg a day lowered perceived stress scores by 44%, versus 5.5% on placebo, and blood cortisol by 27.9%, versus 7.9% [1].
- **Shoden extract, 8 weeks:** 60 adults, 23 of them women, took 240 mg a day. This trial reported women separately. Morning cortisol fell 25% in women taking ashwagandha, versus 0.6% on placebo. Testosterone didn't change in women [2].
- **Root-and-leaf extract, 8 weeks:** 131 stressed adults, 40 of them women. Stress measures improved more than with placebo [12].

So the main stress findings are not men-only data. But no trial was large enough to show whether women respond differently from men.

## Sleep

A 2021 meta-analysis of 5 trials (400 adults) found a small overall improvement in sleep. It was larger in people with insomnia, at 600 mg a day or more, and after 8 weeks or more [3]. The trials enrolled both men and women. For the timeline, see [how long does ashwagandha take to work?](/blog/how-long-does-ashwagandha-take-to-work)

## Menopause symptom scores

Two trials have tested ashwagandha alone in women around menopause:

- **Perimenopause, 8 weeks:** 100 women with menopause symptoms took 300 mg of root extract twice a day or placebo, and 91 finished. Menopause Rating Scale scores and quality-of-life scores improved more than with placebo. Estradiol rose, and FSH and LH (hormones that rise around menopause) fell, compared with placebo [4].
- **After menopause, 12 weeks:** 66 women aged 45–65 took 600 mg a day of root extract or placebo, and 63 finished. Sleep-quality scores improved by 5.2 points versus 0.8, and menopause symptom scores by 8.1 points versus 1.2. Bone density didn't differ [5].

Both trials were single-country and short. NCCIH says there isn't enough evidence to know whether ashwagandha helps with menopause [8]. The hormone shifts in the first trial are worth noting too: their long-term meaning isn't known.

## Sexual function: small studies, read with care

Two small trials of KSM-66 (300 mg twice a day for 8 weeks) enrolled women with low scores on the Female Sexual Function Index, a questionnaire:

- **50 women, 2015:** total scores rose to 23.9 with ashwagandha versus 20.1 with placebo. Desire and pain scores didn't differ. The extract's maker supplied it and covered some costs [6].
- **80 women, 2022:** scores rose by 8.4 points versus 5.1 on placebo [7].

In both, the placebo groups also improved a lot. The 2015 authors wrote that their results shouldn't be read as showing ashwagandha is an aphrodisiac [6]. Two small, sponsor-linked trials aren't enough to draw firm conclusions, and we make no claims here.

## Thyroid: the biggest caution for women

Ashwagandha can raise thyroid hormones. In an 8-week trial of 50 adults with raised TSH (a thyroid marker), 600 mg a day of root extract raised T3 and T4 and lowered TSH compared with placebo [9]. In a small trial in people with bipolar disorder, T4 rose in all 3 people tested [13].

NCCIH says ashwagandha isn't recommended for people with thyroid or autoimmune disorders, and it may interact with thyroid hormone medicines [8]. If you have a thyroid condition or take levothyroxine, talk to your doctor first.

## Liver: rare but serious

LiverTox, the NIH database on drug-induced liver injury, says ashwagandha has been implicated in cases of clinically apparent liver injury [10]. In a series of 5 cases from Iceland and the US (2 of them women, aged about 60), jaundice began 2–12 weeks after starting it. Nobody developed liver failure, and in most, liver tests returned to normal within 1–5 months [14].

Stop and see a doctor if you notice yellowing of the skin or eyes, itching, nausea, unusual tiredness or stomach pain [14].

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding: avoid

- **Pregnancy:** NCCIH says to avoid ashwagandha during pregnancy [8]. The Dutch risk assessor RIVM notes it was traditionally used to induce abortion and that this effect hasn't been studied, so it advises against it in pregnancy as a precaution [15].
- **Breastfeeding:** there are no data on whether it passes into breast milk or on safety for nursing babies. LactMed, the NIH lactation database, says it should be avoided, especially while nursing a newborn or preterm baby [11]. NCCIH agrees [8].
- **Trying to conceive:** trials excluded pregnant women, and some required contraception, so there's little data [2]. Ask your doctor before starting.

## Interactions

NCCIH lists possible interactions with [8]:

- **Sedatives** and **anti-seizure medicines** (drowsiness is a known side effect)
- **Thyroid hormone** medicines
- **Immunosuppressants**
- **Diabetes** and **blood-pressure** medicines

Germany's risk assessor BfR also advises people with liver disease to avoid it [16]. If you take any regular medicine, check with your doctor or pharmacist.

## How to take it, if you do

- **Use a named extract at its studied dose,** such as 600 mg a day of KSM-66. The full evidence and doses are in our [ashwagandha guide](/guides/ashwagandha).
- **Give it about 8 weeks.** That's when most trials measured results [1][3].
- **Don't use it long term without advice.** NCCIH says it may be safe for up to 3 months; longer-term safety isn't known [8].
