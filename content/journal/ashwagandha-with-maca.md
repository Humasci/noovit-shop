**Short answer:** we found **no human trial that tested ashwagandha and maca together**. So a blend's evidence comes from each ingredient on its own, at the dose used in its own studies. In the blend we sell, Ashwagandha Calm+, the 600 mg of KSM-66 ashwagandha matches the main stress and sleep trials [1][2]. Maca, ginseng and shatavari are supporting ingredients at **well below** the doses used in their studies. We'd rather say that plainly than let the label imply otherwise.

## Why blends are hard to judge

Each herb's research was done on one preparation at one dose. As the NIH's LactMed database puts it, testing results on one product may not apply to other products [3]. Adding herbs together also means more things that could cause a side effect or interact with a medicine. So the fair way to read a blend is ingredient by ingredient.

Evidence grades: Moderate = several good human trials; Limited = few, small or mixed trials; Early = very few human studies. An ingredient used well below its studied dose has no direct evidence at that dose.

## Ashwagandha: the main ingredient

This is the part with real evidence behind it. In a 60-day trial of 64 stressed adults, 600 mg a day of KSM-66 lowered perceived stress scores by 44%, versus 5.5% on placebo, and cortisol by 27.9%, versus 7.9% [1]. A meta-analysis of 5 trials found a small improvement in sleep, larger at 600 mg a day or more and after 8 weeks or more [2]. Most trials were small and short. Full details are in our [ashwagandha guide](/guides/ashwagandha), and how it compares with another extract is in [KSM-66 vs Sensoril](/blog/ksm-66-vs-sensoril).

## Maca: small trials, mixed results

Maca is a root from the Andes, in the brassica (mustard) family [13].

- **Sexual desire in men:** in a 12-week trial, 1,500 or 3,000 mg a day of maca improved self-rated sexual desire from week 8, with no change in testosterone or estradiol [4].
- **After menopause:** in a 6-week crossover trial of 14 women, 3,500 mg a day lowered scores for psychological symptoms and sexual problems on a menopause questionnaire. Hormone levels didn't change [5].
- **The reviews:** a systematic review of 4 trials on sexual function found "limited evidence." One of the 4, in cyclists, found no effect [13]. A second review of 4 trials on menopause symptoms also found limited evidence and said maca's safety hasn't been proven [14].

**At 150 mg**, a blend gives roughly a tenth to a twentieth of the studied amounts. Treat maca here as a supporting ingredient, not as the dose from these trials.

## Panax ginseng

Panax (Asian) ginseng has more research than maca, but much of it is mixed. NCCIH says it may improve some aspects of thinking in middle-aged adults, may have a small effect on general fatigue, and doesn't seem to improve athletic performance [15]. A Cochrane review found no convincing evidence that it improves thinking in healthy people [16]. In one crossover trial, 30 young adults took single doses of 200 or 400 mg of a standardized ginseng extract; the 200 mg dose improved a mental arithmetic task and lowered self-rated mental fatigue [6].

The 100 mg in a blend is plain root powder, not that standardized extract, so the doses aren't directly comparable.

**Safety:** insomnia is the most common side effect. Ginseng may lower blood sugar, may worsen autoimmune conditions and may affect blood clotting. Some experts advise against it in pregnancy and while breastfeeding [15].

## Shatavari: very little human data

Shatavari (Asparagus racemosus) is an Ayurvedic root, not the asparagus you eat [17]. Human trials are few:

- In a 24-week trial in postmenopausal women (about 20 per group), 250 or 500 mg a day of a shatavari extract improved menopause quality-of-life scores compared with placebo. A supplement company supplied the capsules [7].
- In an 8-week trial of 135 women aged 45–65, 300 mg a day of a shatavari extract improved menopause symptom scores more than placebo, and adding 250 mg of ashwagandha improved them further. The maker of KSM-66 supplied the extracts [8].

LactMed notes that shatavari's safety has not been rigorously studied [17]. At 50 mg of root powder, a blend contains far less than these trials used.

## L-arginine and the vitamins

**L-arginine** is an amino acid found in protein foods. Trials on blood pressure used 4–24 g a day [9]; 300 mg is a small fraction of that. One safety signal matters: in a trial in people who had just had a heart attack, 9 g a day of L-arginine didn't help, and 6 people on it died versus none on placebo. The authors said it shouldn't be recommended after a heart attack [18].

**The vitamins** are at normal daily amounts. 20 mcg of D3 is the daily need for adults over 70 and a little above the 15 mcg for younger adults, well under the 100 mcg upper limit [10]. B6 and B12 are above daily needs [11][12]. If you already take a multivitamin or vitamin D, add up your total.

## Should you choose a blend or single ingredients?

- **Choose a single-ingredient ashwagandha** if you want exactly what the trials tested, or if you're sensitive to supplements, so any side effect is easier to trace.
- **A blend can make sense** if you want the studied ashwagandha dose plus everyday vitamins in one product, and you accept that the extra herbs are there in small, supporting amounts.
- **Either way,** the ashwagandha cautions apply: avoid it in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and talk to your doctor first if you have a thyroid or liver condition or take sedatives, thyroid, diabetes, blood-pressure or immune-suppressing medicines [19].
