**Short answer:** ferritin is the protein that stores iron, so a low ferritin result means your iron stores are low [1]. Many people feel nothing. Others notice tiredness, poor concentration, restless legs, cravings for ice or other non-foods, breathlessness or lower exercise tolerance [2]. These signs are vague and have many other causes, so the only way to know is a blood test. If yours is low, see a doctor to find out **why**, rather than just starting iron [2].

## What ferritin is

Ferritin binds iron and stores it in the body, much of it in the liver [1]. A small amount circulates in the blood, and that level tracks your stores. That's why ferritin is the main test for iron deficiency in otherwise healthy people [3].

Iron deficiency happens in stages. Stores run down first. Only later does hemoglobin fall and anemia appear [2]. So you can have low ferritin with a normal blood count. A 2025 review in JAMA estimates that about 38% of non-pregnant women of reproductive age in high-income countries have iron deficiency without anemia, and about 13% have iron-deficiency anemia [2].

## Symptoms of low iron stores

The same JAMA review lists these signs of iron deficiency, with or without anemia [2]:

- **Tiredness**, irritability and low mood
- **Difficulty concentrating**
- **Restless legs** (reported in 32–40% of people with iron deficiency)
- **Pica**, cravings for ice or non-food items (40–50%)
- **Breathlessness, lightheadedness and lower exercise tolerance**

It also notes that some people have **no symptoms at all** [2].

**Tiredness** is the best-studied symptom. In a French trial of 198 menstruating women with unexplained fatigue, normal hemoglobin and ferritin below 50 µg/L, 80 mg of iron a day for 12 weeks cut fatigue scores by 47.7%, versus 28.8% on placebo. Quality of life and mood scores didn't differ [4]. A review of 18 trials found that iron eased self-reported fatigue in iron-deficient adults without anemia. It didn't improve objective fitness measures such as VO2 max [5].

**Hair shedding** is often linked to low iron, but the evidence is mixed. A dermatology review found that some studies tie iron deficiency to hair loss and others don't. It found too little evidence to recommend iron for hair loss without anemia [6]. Rapid weight loss and low protein can also trigger shedding (see our [GLP-1 nutrition guide](/guides/glp-1-nutrition)).

## Ferritin cut-offs: why labs and doctors differ

| Cut-off | Who uses it | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Below 15 µg/L | WHO threshold for women [7] | Iron stores are used up. It's based on expert opinion, last revised in the 1990s [7][8] |
| About 25 µg/L | A US study of NHANES data [7] | The level at which iron-poor red blood cell production starts in non-pregnant women |
| Below 30 µg/L (30 ng/mL) | Common clinical cut-off [2] | Usual definition of iron deficiency when there's no inflammation |
| Below 70 µg/L | WHO, adults with infection or inflammation [3] | Inflammation raises ferritin, so a "normal" result can hide low stores |

Note that 1 µg/L equals 1 ng/mL, so lab reports may use either unit.

**Why inflammation matters:** ferritin rises with infection, surgery, liver disease and other conditions [1]. So a mid-range result doesn't always mean your stores are fine. Doctors may add a transferrin saturation test (below 20% suggests iron deficiency) or a CRP test for inflammation [2][3].

## Who's most at risk

- **Menstruating women**, especially with heavy periods. Bleeding is the most common cause of iron deficiency [2].
- **Pregnancy.** In high-income countries, up to 84% of women are iron deficient by the third trimester [2]. Iron needs rise to 27 mg a day, from 18 mg [9].
- **Plant-based diets.** Iron from plants is harder to absorb [9]. A meta-analysis of 24 studies found vegetarians had ferritin about 30 µg/L lower than meat eaters [10].
- **Endurance athletes.** About 15–35% of female and 5–11% of male athlete groups are iron deficient [11].
- **Frequent blood donors.** In a US study of donors, 27% of women who gave blood often had no iron stores left by their last visit [12].
- **Gut conditions and surgery** that cut absorption, such as celiac disease and bariatric surgery, and regular use of NSAID painkillers [2].

## Why test before taking iron

**Iron builds up.** The body has no real way to get rid of extra iron, so absorption is its only control [13]. Taking iron when you don't need it can lead to overload [6].

**Hemochromatosis is common.** This inherited condition makes the body absorb too much iron. It affects about 1 million people in the US, most often people of Northern European descent [14]. Doctors may tell people who have it to avoid iron and vitamin C supplements [15]. WHO notes that ferritin above 150 µg/L in menstruating women, or above 200 µg/L in men and other women, may signal a risk of iron overload [3].

**Iron is dangerous for children.** US law requires this warning on iron supplements: accidental overdose is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6 [16]. Keep iron locked away.

**The cause needs finding.** Low ferritin is a sign, not a diagnosis. In men and women after menopause, the usual causes are blood loss from the gut and poor absorption, and doctors may check for conditions such as colon cancer [6]. Guidelines say the cause should always be identified and treated [2].

## How testing works

Ferritin is a simple blood test. A doctor may order it on its own or with other iron tests, and may ask you to fast beforehand [1]. Ask for a copy of your result and the lab's reference range.

At-home kits that use a finger-prick sample are also sold. In one small validation study of a point-of-care device, finger-prick results tracked lab results closely, but agreement was weaker at higher levels [17]. Whichever route you take, go over the result with a doctor, especially if it's low.

## Food sources and absorption tips

Good sources include lean red meat, liver, oysters, poultry, fish such as salmon and tuna, eggs, beans, dried fruit, whole grains and iron-fortified cereals [9]. Iron from plants and supplements is harder to absorb than iron from meat [9]. Isotope studies estimate the body absorbs 14–18% of iron from mixed diets, versus 5–12% from vegetarian diets, in people with low stores [13].

- **Pair plant iron with vitamin C.** Citrus, strawberries, tomatoes and potatoes all help absorption [9].
- **Have tea between meals, not with them.** In a small trial in 12 women, tea with a meal cut iron absorption by 37%. Waiting an hour after the meal halved that effect [18]. Polyphenols (found in tea), phytates (in grains and beans) and calcium can all reduce absorption [13].
- **Watch high-dose turmeric.** It may bind iron in the gut; see our [turmeric guide](/guides/turmeric).

## The bottom line

Low ferritin is common, especially in women, and it can make you feel tired, foggy or short of breath. It can also sit quietly with no symptoms. Get tested rather than guessing. If your ferritin is low, see your doctor before taking iron so the cause can be found, and let them set the dose and when to recheck [2].
