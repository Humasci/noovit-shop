**Short answer:** most adult women need **310–320 mg of magnesium a day**, and **44% of US women get less than the average requirement**, even counting supplements [1][2]. Glycinate is a popular way to close that gap because it's less likely to loosen stools than citrate or oxide [1]. The research on specific benefits for women (sleep, cramps, premenstrual symptoms) is small and mixed, so the best reason to take it is simply to meet your daily need. Keep supplements at or below **350 mg a day** unless your doctor advises more [1].

## How much women need

| Life stage | Daily need (RDA) |
|---|---|
| Women 19–30 | 310 mg [1] |
| Women 31 and older | 320 mg [1] |
| Pregnancy, 19–30 | 350 mg [1] |
| Pregnancy, 31–50 | 360 mg [1] |
| Breastfeeding, 19–30 | 310 mg [1] |
| Breastfeeding, 31–50 | 320 mg [1] |

These targets are for total intake from food, drinks and supplements. The separate **upper limit of 350 mg a day** applies only to supplements and medicines, not food. It's set because higher doses can cause diarrhea [1].

## How many women fall short

US survey data from 2015–2018 (NHANES) include supplements, and still show a big gap [2]:

| Group | Typical (median) intake | Below the average requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Women 19–30 | 256 mg | 50% |
| Women 31–50 | 281 mg | 43% |
| Women 51–70 | 287 mg | 41% |
| Women 71+ | 275 mg | 46% |

About 1 in 4 adult women (25%) took a supplement containing magnesium [2]. In a separate table, 52% of pregnant women aged 20–44 were below the average requirement, compared with 44% of women who weren't pregnant or breastfeeding. Only 105 pregnant women were in that sample, so treat that figure with caution [3].

Low intake isn't the same as deficiency. The kidneys hold on to magnesium when intake is low, and clear deficiency is uncommon in healthy people [1]. Blood tests also say little about your stores (more in [can you test magnesium at home?](/blog/can-you-test-magnesium-at-home)). So for most women, the practical question is whether your diet reaches 310–320 mg.

## Why glycinate?

Magnesium glycinate (also called bisglycinate) is magnesium bound to the amino acid glycine. It's the form most often chosen by people who want magnesium without a laxative effect. Citrate and oxide are more likely to loosen stools [1]. No large trial has tested glycinate's gut comfort head to head, though: that reputation comes mostly from small studies and experience. Full comparison: [magnesium glycinate vs citrate vs oxide](/blog/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate-vs-oxide).

Glycinate is only about 14% magnesium by weight, so read the **elemental magnesium** on the label, not the weight of the compound. In one trial, an 893 mg capsule gave 125 mg of magnesium [4].

## Sleep quality

The best-designed recent sleep trial was mostly women. In 2025, 155 adults with self-reported poor sleep took 250 mg of magnesium as bisglycinate, or a placebo, before bed for 4 weeks. Eighty percent were women [4].

- **Result:** insomnia scores fell 3.9 points with magnesium and 2.3 points with placebo. That's a small difference (effect size 0.2) [4].
- **No change** on other sleep, tiredness, stress or mood questionnaires [4].
- The study didn't report results for women and men separately [4].

## Muscle cramps, including in pregnancy

Magnesium is needed for normal muscle and nerve function [1]. But for typical night-time leg cramps, the evidence is weak:

- **In older adults:** a 2020 Cochrane review of 11 trials found magnesium is unlikely to give meaningful relief from typical leg cramps [5].
- **In pregnancy:** five trials in the same review (408 women) gave conflicting results [5]. A separate 2020 Cochrane review of leg cramps in pregnancy found some trials where magnesium helped and others where it made little or no difference. The certainty of the evidence was low or very low [6].

## Premenstrual symptoms

Research on magnesium and premenstrual symptoms exists, but it is small and mixed:

- **A 1991 trial** gave 32 women with confirmed premenstrual symptoms either magnesium or a placebo three times a day in the second half of the cycle, for two cycles. Total symptom scores and "negative mood" scores improved more with magnesium [7]. It was small and short, and the dose (at least 360 mg a day) was above today's 350 mg upper limit [1][7].
- **A 2025 systematic review** of 31 trials of nutrition and premenstrual mood symptoms found **insufficient evidence** for magnesium. Only one of the included trials had a low risk of bias [8].
- **A 2019 meta-analysis** of 13 observational studies found no overall link between blood magnesium levels and premenstrual symptoms [9].

So we can't say magnesium helps with premenstrual symptoms. If your symptoms affect your daily life, talk to your doctor.

## Pregnancy and breastfeeding

- **Needs rise slightly** in pregnancy, to 350–360 mg a day [1].
- **Supplements haven't shown clear benefits.** A Cochrane review of 10 trials (9,090 women) concluded there is not enough high-quality evidence that magnesium supplements in pregnancy are beneficial [10].
- **Check your prenatal vitamin.** Many contain magnesium, and it counts toward the 350 mg supplement limit [1].
- **Ask your doctor or midwife** before adding any supplement while pregnant or breastfeeding.

## Safety and interactions

- **Side effects:** diarrhea, nausea and stomach cramps, mostly at higher doses [1]. In the 2025 trial, side effects were uncommon (2 people on magnesium reported one, versus 7 on placebo) [4].
- **Kidney disease:** don't take magnesium supplements unless your doctor prescribes them. Excess magnesium can build up [1].
- **Osteoporosis drugs (bisphosphonates, such as alendronate):** take magnesium at least 2 hours before or after them [1].
- **Antibiotics:** take tetracyclines (such as doxycycline) or quinolones (such as ciprofloxacin) at least 2 hours before, or 4–6 hours after, magnesium [1].
- **Acid reducers (PPIs)** used for more than a year and **some diuretics** can change magnesium levels. Ask your doctor whether to check yours [1].
- **Add it all up:** multivitamins, prenatal vitamins, antacids and laxatives can all contain magnesium [1].

## The bottom line

Many women don't get enough magnesium, and glycinate is a gentle way to top up. Start with food: pumpkin seeds, chia seeds, almonds, spinach and black beans are all good sources [1]. If you add a supplement, keep it at or below 350 mg a day, and take it for your overall intake rather than to fix a specific symptom.
