# Perimenopause, Decoded: medical basics (research notes)

Research for the 6-week email program. Researched 23 Nov 2026. Every claim below comes from a source opened and read in full
or in the cited section. Source numbers [S#] refer to the list at the end. Education only: nothing here says or implies that
a supplement treats anything.

## 1. What perimenopause is

- **Perimenopause is the time around menopause.** It starts when cycles first become irregular and ends 12 months after the
  final period. STRAW+10: perimenopause "begins at Stage −2 and ends 12 months after the final menstrual period" [S1].
- **Menopause is a single point: 12 months in a row with no period.** "You reach menopause after 12 months in a row with no
  period, including no spotting" [S7]. ACOG: "Menopause is confirmed after 1 year of no periods" [S5].
- **Postmenopause is everything after that point.** STRAW+10 puts early postmenopause at about 5 to 8 years [S1].
- **Typical age.** "Perimenopause usually begins in your mid- to late 40s. However, some women notice symptoms in their mid- to
  late 30s" [S7]. ACOG: "Perimenopause may last from ages 45 to 55, although the timing varies" [S5]. Santoro: for the average
  woman, early transition at 47, late transition at 49, final period at 51 [S4].
- **Average age of menopause: 51 to 52.** ACOG says 51 [S5, S6]; the HHS Office on Women's Health says 52 [S7]. Sources differ
  slightly; "around 51–52" is safe.
- **Duration.** "Perimenopause lasts about four years on average but can last up to 10 years" [S7]. Earlier onset may mean a
  longer transition: "earlier onset of the transition may herald a longer and therefore more bothersome one" [S4].
- **Pregnancy is still possible.** "You can get pregnant during perimenopause, even if you skip periods" [S7].

**STRAW+10 stages in plain words** [S1] (Harlow et al. 2012):
- **Early transition (Stage −2):** cycle length starts to vary. Definition: "a persistent difference of 7 days or more" in
  length between consecutive cycles, recurring "within 10 cycles" of the first variable cycle.
- **Late transition (Stage −1):** you start skipping periods for long stretches: "amenorrhea of 60 days or longer". A
  supportive (research) criterion is FSH "greater than 25 IU/L in a random blood draw". This stage lasts on average "1 to 3
  years".
- These are research criteria. In practice, diagnosis is based on age, symptoms and period changes (see section 2).

## 2. Hormones, simply

- **Hormones swing; they don't fall smoothly.** SWAN found estradiol "decreased across the FMP beginning at 2 years before the
  FMP" and stabilized about 2 years after. FSH rises slowly from about 7 years before the final period, then sharply from 2
  years before to 2 years after [S3].
- **Not everyone follows the same pattern.** SWAN estradiol patterns: 31.5% had a rise about 5.5 years before the final period
  then a steep fall; 13.1% a rise then slow fall; 26.9% a slow decline; 28.6% a flat, limited decline [S3].
- **Estrogen is erratic.** Santoro reports "erratic estrogen secretory patterns" in the transition; estrogen "is more likely to be
  low during anovulatory cycles", but some such cycles have "relatively high estrogen levels" [S4].
- **Progesterone drops because ovulation becomes less regular.** Santoro reports "lower luteal progesterone and higher FSH"
  [S4]. Some months there is no ovulation (ACOG: "Some months, the ovaries may release an egg. Other months, they do not") [S5].
- **FSH is often normal early on.** In the early transition FSH "is often normal and only occasionally elevated" [S4]. The
  process "is not simply a loss of estrogen accompanied by elevated FSH, at least not in its earlier stages" [S4].

**Why a single blood test usually doesn't help (women 45+)**
- NICE NG23 (UK, last updated 15 Apr 2026), rec 1.3.1: identify perimenopause "without laboratory tests" in otherwise healthy
  people aged 45 or over "if they have vasomotor symptoms that have recently started and any changes in their menstrual cycle"
  [S8].
- Rec 1.3.4: "Do not use the following laboratory and imaging tests to identify perimenopause or menopause in people aged 45 or
  over: anti-Müllerian hormone, inhibin A, inhibin B, oestradiol, antral follicle count, ovarian volume" [S8].
- Rec 1.3.5: do not use FSH "in people using combined oestrogen and progestogen contraception or high-dose progestogen" [S8].
- US government (OWH): "Your health care provider can usually determine whether you are in perimenopause from your age, your
  symptoms, and changes in your periods. Blood hormone tests are not usually needed" [S7].
- MedlinePlus notes that if periods haven't stopped, "Your provider may need to repeat testing your hormone levels several
  times" [S9]. This fits the erratic pattern above.

**When testing can help**
- NICE rec 1.3.6: consider FSH "only" in people aged 40 to 45 with symptoms including cycle change, and in people under 40 in
  whom menopause is suspected [S8].
- Under 40 (suspected premature ovarian insufficiency): diagnose on symptoms plus "elevated FSH levels on 2 blood samples taken 4
  to 6 weeks apart"; "Do not diagnose ... on the basis of a single blood test" [S8].
- OWH: "If your periods stop before age 40, you can ask about testing" [S7].
- Note: tests for other causes of symptoms (e.g. thyroid) are a separate question; not covered by these sources.

## 3. Common symptoms and how common they are

- **Many women have few or no symptoms; others have severe ones.** ACOG: "Some women do not have any symptoms ... Others have
  many symptoms that can be severe" [S6].
- **Hot flashes and night sweats.** "Up to 80%" of SWAN women reported them at some point in the transition [S3].
  - SWAN (Avis 2015, 1,449 women with frequent symptoms, i.e. "at least 6 days in the previous 2 weeks"): **median total
    duration 7.4 years**; median 4.5 years after the final period [S2].
  - Starting early matters most: onset when premenopausal or early perimenopausal → median total duration "above 11.8 years";
    onset after menopause → 3.4 years [S2].
  - **By ethnicity (median total duration):** African American 10.1 y, Hispanic 8.9 y, non-Hispanic White 6.5 y, Chinese
    5.4 y, Japanese 4.8 y [S2]. Black women had the highest prevalence and were most bothered; Asian women the lowest
    prevalence [S3].
  - The 2023 Menopause Society statement: symptoms "last a mean duration of 7 to 9 years, longer in some women" [S11]. OWH says
    "7 to 12 years on average" [S7]. Use "often 7 years or more".
  - A single hot flash "may last from a few seconds to several minutes or longer" [S6].
- **Sleep.** Late perimenopausal and postmenopausal women reported more sleep difficulty than premenopausal women in SWAN
  cross-sectional data. But in trajectory analysis "only 15%" had sleep complaints rising with the final period [S3]. (No
  single prevalence figure found in an opened source.)
- **Mood and depression: a window of higher risk.** The Menopause Society/NNDC guideline calls perimenopause "a window of
  vulnerability for the development of both depressive symptoms and major depressive episodes" [S13]. SWAN: odds of high
  depressive symptoms were higher in early perimenopause, late perimenopause and postmenopause than premenopause, highest in
  late perimenopause; women were "two to four times as likely" to have a major depressive episode in the transition or early
  postmenopause [S3]. **Nuance:** "most midlife women who experience a major depressive episode during the perimenopause have
  experienced a prior episode" [S13]; in SWAN, menopausal status did not significantly predict a *first* episode [S3].
- **Brain fog and memory.** Roughly 60% of midlife women report memory problems during the transition [S3]. SWAN found a
  "temporary decrement" in processing speed and verbal memory in perimenopause that resolved after menopause (an absence of the
  usual practice gain rather than a drop) [S3]. OWH: these changes "often improve after menopause" [S10].
- **Irregular periods.** Cycles may be "shorter or longer", flow "heavier or lighter", and periods may be skipped [S5].
- **Vaginal dryness / genitourinary syndrome (GSM).** In SWAN, vaginal dryness rose from about 19% (pre/early peri) to 34%
  (postmenopause) [S3]. GSM can include repeat UTIs, pain with sex, burning or itching, urgency and dryness [S10]. Without
  treatment it "may get worse over time" [S7].
- **Joint and muscle aches.** Meta-analysis of 16 studies (mostly cross-sectional): musculoskeletal pain in 71% of perimenopausal
  women; odds 1.63× higher than premenopause; no significant difference vs postmenopause [S15]. OWH cites a 37-study review: 57%
  in perimenopause vs 40% before [S10]. **Uncertainty:** high heterogeneity, self-report, possible publication bias; age itself
  may explain part of this [S15].
- **Palpitations.** SWAN (3,276 women): 15.9% had a high-probability trajectory, 34.3% moderate, 49.8% low; palpitations are
  "common from early perimenopause through early postmenopause" [S16]. They were not linked to subclinical heart disease after
  full adjustment [S16]. (New palpitations still deserve a medical check.)

## 4. Long-term health

- **Bone loss speeds up around the final period.** SWAN (Greendale 2012): loss runs fastest from 1 year before to 2 years after
  the final period: **−2.46%/year at the lumbar spine** and **−1.76%/year at the femoral neck** (White reference group) [S14].
  Over 10 years: spine −10.6% (7.38% of it in that window), femoral neck −9.1% [S14]. Loss slows but continues after (about
  −1.1%/year) [S14]. Higher BMI and African American heritage were tied to slower loss; Japanese and Chinese women had faster
  femoral-neck loss [S14].
- **ACOG:** "during the first 4–8 years after menopause, women lose bone more rapidly" [S6].
- **Cholesterol and arteries.** SWAN: total cholesterol, LDL-C and ApoB rise sharply around the final period; carotid wall
  thickening accelerates in late perimenopause; odds of metabolic syndrome were highest in perimenopause [S3]. Blood pressure,
  glucose and BMI were *not* tied to the transition after adjusting for age [S3].
- **Muscle and fat.** About 2 years before the final period, fat gain doubles and lean mass starts to fall; both level off
  about 2 years after [S3]. Patterns differed by ethnicity [S3].

## 5. What doctors can offer (neutral)

**Hormone therapy (2022 Menopause Society statement [S12])**
- "Hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms (VMS) and the genitourinary syndrome of menopause
  and has been shown to prevent bone loss and fracture."
- Timing: "For women aged younger than 60 years or who are within 10 years of menopause onset and have no contraindications, the
  benefit-risk ratio is favorable for treatment of bothersome VMS and prevention of bone loss."
- "For women who initiate hormone therapy more than 10 years from menopause onset or who are aged older than 60 years, the
  benefit-risk ratio appears less favorable because of the greater absolute risks of coronary heart disease, stroke, venous
  thromboembolism, and dementia."
- Risks depend on "type, dose, duration of use, route of administration, timing of initiation, and whether a progestogen is
  used." Women with a uterus need a progestogen to protect the lining (Level I). Absolute risks are "rare (<10/10,000/y)".
- Contraindications include "unexplained vaginal bleeding; liver disease; prior estrogen-sensitive cancer ...; prior coronary heart
  disease, stroke, MI, or VTE".
- For genitourinary symptoms not helped by over-the-counter products: "low-dose vaginal estrogen therapy or other therapies (eg,
  vaginal dehydroepiandrosterone or oral ospemifene) are recommended."
- Compounded "bioidentical" hormones: insufficient evidence for overall clinical use [S12]; ACOG "recommends FDA-approved hormone
  therapy over compounded hormone therapy" [S6].
- **Labeling update:** in November 2025 FDA requested removal of heart disease, breast cancer and probable dementia statements
  from the boxed warning; in February 2026 it approved this for six products. The endometrial cancer boxed warning remains on
  systemic estrogen-only products [S17]. OWH: HT "should not be taken just to prevent heart disease" [S17].

**Non-hormonal options (2023 Menopause Society statement [S11])**
Evidence levels: "Level I, good and consistent scientific evidence; Level II, limited or inconsistent scientific evidence, and
Level III, consensus and expert opinion."

*Recommended:*
- CBT, clinical hypnosis, SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, fezolinetant — **Level I**
- Oxybutynin — **Levels I–II** (dry mouth, urinary difficulty; long-term anticholinergic use may be linked to cognitive decline in
  older people)
- Weight loss, stellate ganglion block — **Levels II–III**
- Low-dose paroxetine 7.5 mg/day "was the first nonhormone pharmaceutical FDA approved" for moderate to severe VMS. Fezolinetant
  (45 mg/day) is a neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist.

*Not recommended* ("negative or insufficient evidence"):
- Paced respiration — **Level I**
- Supplements/herbal remedies — **Levels I–II** (e.g. black cohosh: "insufficient evidence", Level I; vitamin E, Level I)
- Cooling techniques, avoiding triggers, exercise, yoga, mindfulness-based intervention, relaxation, suvorexant, soy foods and soy
  extracts, equol, cannabinoids, acupuncture, calibration of neural oscillations — **Level II**
- Chiropractic interventions, clonidine — **Levels I–III**
- Dietary modification, pregabalin — **Level III**
- Important nuance: exercise and yoga are not recommended *for hot flashes* only. The statement notes "other health benefits
  associated with exercise or yoga" and that "A healthy diet is important for health promotion" [S11]. ACOG recommends
  weight-bearing and strength exercise for bone health [S6].
- Conflict to flag: ACOG's patient page still lists clonidine as a hot-flash option [S6]; the 2023 statement does not recommend
  it [S11].

**Since 2023**
- FDA now lists three approved non-hormone medicines for moderate to severe hot flashes: low-dose paroxetine, fezolinetant and
  elinzanetant [S10b]. SSRIs/SNRIs (other than low-dose paroxetine) and gabapentin are used off-label [S10b].
- Fezolinetant: FDA warning (12 Sep 2024), then boxed warning (16 Dec 2024) for "rare but serious liver injury"; liver tests
  before starting, monthly for the first 3 months, then at months 6 and 9 [S18].
- Depression: antidepressants and psychotherapy are "the front-line treatments for perimenopausal depression"; estrogen is not
  approved for it, though it has shown antidepressant effects "particularly those with concomitant vasomotor symptoms" [S13].

## 6. When to see a doctor (red flags)

From ACOG [S5] unless noted. Period changes are normal, but tell a clinician about:
- "bleeding or spotting between periods"
- "bleeding or spotting after sex"
- "heavy bleeding during your period"; "bleeding that is heavier or lasts for more days than usual"
- **any bleeding after menopause**: "It's especially important to tell your ob-gyn if you have bleeding after menopause." OWH:
  "Vaginal bleeding after 12 months with no period is not normal. See your health care provider right away" [S10]. Bleeding "is
  the most common sign of endometrial cancer in postmenopausal women" [S5].
- Causes ACOG lists include polyps, endometrial atrophy, endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer [S5].
- Periods stopping **before age 40** (ask about testing) [S7].
- **Thoughts of self-harm:** "call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline" [S10].
- New or worsening joint pain; trouble sleeping (insomnia or sleep apnea are possible) [S10].
- Note: I did not find an opened US source giving a numeric definition of "very heavy" bleeding; keep wording qualitative.

## 7. Talking to your doctor

- **Track periods and symptoms.** "Track your periods and symptoms and share the information with your provider" [S7].
- **Bring** (OWH [S10]): "Your symptoms and how much they bother you"; "Your health history, including cancer, blood clots, and
  heart disease"; "Any hormone therapy you have used"; "How long it has been since your last period". Also say which symptoms
  "bother you most".
- **Questions to ask about hormone therapy** (OWH [S17]): "Which of my symptoms can hormone therapy treat?"; "Is hormone therapy
  safe for me, given my age and health history?"; "Which type of hormone therapy, and which way of taking it, is best for me?";
  "How long will I need to be on hormone therapy? How often should my hormone treatment be reviewed?"; "Are there other
  treatments that can help my symptoms?"
- Ask about **birth control** needs [S7].
- **Tell the clinician about any supplements** you take; ACOG notes these products' manufacture "is not regulated" for content
  and dose and "be sure to let your health care professional know" [S6].
- Treatment decisions are "shared decisions" [S10b].

## Key takeaways for the emails

1. Perimenopause is a transition, often about 4 years and up to 10; menopause is one day, 12 months after the last period.
2. It usually starts in the mid-to-late 40s; average menopause is around 51–52.
3. Hormones swing up and down; they don't fall in a straight line. That is why one blood test is often unhelpful after 45.
4. Diagnosis after 45 is usually based on age, symptoms and period changes; testing matters more under 45.
5. Up to 80% of women get hot flashes; when frequent, they last a median of 7.4 years, longer if they start early and for
   Black and Hispanic women.
6. Sleep, mood, memory, joint aches, palpitations and vaginal dryness are all common. Memory dips tend to recover after
   menopause.
7. Perimenopause is a higher-risk window for depression, especially with a past history. Use 988 in a crisis.
8. Bone loss is fastest from 1 year before to 2 years after the last period (~2.5%/year at the spine); cholesterol rises and
   lean mass falls in the same window.
9. Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for hot flashes; benefit-risk is favorable for most healthy women under 60
   or within 10 years of menopause.
10. Proven non-hormone options exist: CBT, clinical hypnosis, and several prescription drugs.
11. Experts do not recommend supplements, soy, cooling or exercise *for hot flashes* (exercise still helps bones and health).
12. Bleeding after 12 months without a period, between periods or after sex, or much heavier bleeding, needs a doctor.
