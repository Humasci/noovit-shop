# Perimenopause, Decoded: what helps (research notes)

Researched 23 Nov 2026 for the 6-week email program. These are internal notes, not copy. Sources are cited as [n] and listed with titles and URLs at the end.

**Method.** Every claim comes from a source opened in this session. I read two sources in full: the 2023 Menopause Society statement [1] and the 2026 magnesium review [14]. For other journal papers I read the abstract (design, numbers, conclusion) through Europe PMC, not the full paper. I read the NCCIH and MedlinePlus pages in full.

**Could not open:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements (ODS) fact sheets (HTTP 403; archive copies unreachable), PubMed (cookie wall, so Europe PMC records were used instead), NCBI Bookshelf/LiverTox (captcha) and the journal's own page for [1] (paywall; a university-hosted PDF of the same article was used). Numbers usually taken from ODS come from the other sources named here.

**Grades.** Moderate = several good human trials. Limited = few or small trials, or mixed results. Early = lab, biomarker or very small pilot only. Insufficient = trials found no clear effect, or there are no trials. **NAMS levels** are the levels used in [1]: I = good, consistent evidence; II = limited or inconsistent; III = consensus or expert opinion.

## A. Lifestyle

### 1. Sleep
**CBT for insomnia (CBT-I).** In a trial of 106 peri- and postmenopausal women aged 40–65 with insomnia and hot flashes, six phone sessions over 8 weeks cut insomnia scores by 9.9 points, vs 4.7 with menopause education (a 5.2-point difference on a 0–28 scale). At 24 weeks, 84% of the CBT-I group (37 of 44) were in the no-insomnia range, vs 43% (16 of 37) of controls [2].
- **Null result:** the number of daily hot flashes did not change.
- The MsFLASH network summary confirms the benefit for insomnia and sleep quality [3]. [1] notes that women with both hot flashes and insomnia did best with CBT-I in pooled data.
- **Grade: Moderate**, in peri- and postmenopausal women.

**Sleep hygiene.** In [2], sleep hygiene was only one part of CBT-I, alongside sleep restriction, stimulus control and cognitive work. I found no trial of sleep hygiene on its own in menopausal women. [1] cites only one small (n = 20), uncontrolled study that paired a cooling device with sleep hygiene advice.
- **Grade: Limited** as a stand-alone tool.

### 2. Hot flashes: the 2023 position statement [1]
[1] also states that hormone therapy remains the most effective treatment for hot flashes.

**CBT: recommended, Level I.** It lowers how much hot flashes bother and disrupt women. It does not consistently lower how often they happen.
- In MENOS 2 (140 peri- and postmenopausal women), 65–78% of women in the CBT arms reached a clinically meaningful improvement. Gains held at 26 weeks.
- **Caveat:** most trials compared CBT with waitlist or usual care, not with an active control.
- **Grade: Moderate**, in menopausal women and breast cancer survivors.

**Clinical hypnosis: recommended, Level I.**
- In 187 postmenopausal women with 50 or more hot flashes a week, 12 weeks of hypnosis cut reported frequency by 74%, vs 17% with an attention control. Hot flashes measured by a monitor fell 57% vs 10%.
- A second trial was in 60 breast cancer survivors. Hypnosis can be delivered by a trained provider or through an app.
- NCCIH reports a similar NIH-funded finding [30c].
- **Grade: Moderate**, but based on only two trials.

**Cooling (layers, fans, cold packs, a cooler room): not recommended, Level II.**
- These have not been properly tested. In a 4-week trial (n = 39), a night-time cooling device did not change measured night sweats, although women said they slept better.
- **Grade: Insufficient** as a treatment. Fine to mention as comfort steps, but not as a proven treatment.

**Avoiding triggers (alcohol, caffeine, spicy or hot food): not recommended, Level II.**
- No clinical trials exist. One study of 4,595 women linked alcohol with hot flashes, but another cohort did not.
- **Grade: Insufficient.**

**Weight loss: recommended, Levels II–III.**
- Behavioral weight-loss trials show fewer hot flashes, possibly more so earlier in the transition. The studies are small, pilots, or secondary analyses.
- **Grade: Limited.**

**Also not recommended for hot flashes:** paced breathing (Level I); exercise, yoga, mindfulness and relaxation (Level II); dietary changes (Level III).

### 3. Strength training, bone and muscle; exercise, sleep and mood
**Bone.** The LIFTMOR trial [4] studied 101 postmenopausal women (mean age 65) with low bone mass for 8 months.
- One group did twice-weekly, 30-minute, **supervised** high-intensity resistance and impact training (5×5 at >85% of max). The other did a home-based, low-intensity program.
- Spine bone density rose 2.9% vs fell 1.2%. Femoral neck density changed +0.3% vs −1.9%. All function tests improved more in the training group.
- There was one minor adverse event.
- **Safety:** the sessions were closely supervised.
- **Grade: Limited** (one key RCT), in postmenopausal women with low bone mass.

**Muscle.** A meta-analysis of 14 RCTs in postmenopausal or older women found about 1.3 kg more lean mass with higher-volume training and 0.9 kg with lower-volume training, vs controls. The reviewers rated certainty of evidence as low [5].
- **Grade: Moderate** (many trials).

**Sleep.** A meta-analysis of 17 RCTs (10 pooled) in menopausal women found that exercise reduced insomnia severity (SMD −0.91) [6].
- **Null result:** sleep quality did not differ significantly.
- Effects were larger in women who already had sleep problems.
- An earlier meta-analysis (4 RCTs, 660 midlife women) found a small gain in sleep quality with moderate aerobic exercise but not with yoga [7].
- **Grade: Limited.**

**Mood.** A meta-analysis of 21 RCTs in 1,398 Chinese perimenopausal women found fewer depressive symptoms with exercise (SMD −1.37). Heterogeneity was high, and all trials were in one country [8].
- **Grade: Limited.**

### 4. Protein
PROT-AGE (European geriatric societies) advises people **over 65** to eat at least **1.0–1.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day** [9].
- **≥1.2 g/kg** for people who exercise or are active.
- **1.2–1.5 g/kg** for most people with acute or chronic illness.
- People with severe kidney disease (eGFR <30) who are not on dialysis may need less.
- The group says the evidence is not yet good enough for advice on timing or protein quality.

I found no midlife-specific number in an opened source. The ESPEN geriatrics abstract I opened gave no protein figure.
- **Status:** expert consensus for over-65s.

### 5. Caffeine and alcohol
- **Caffeine and sleep:** a meta-analysis of 24 studies found that caffeine cut total sleep by 45 minutes and sleep efficiency by 7%, and added 9 minutes to the time it took to fall asleep. The authors suggest having coffee at least 8.8 hours before bed [10]. **Grade: Moderate**, in general adults.
- **Caffeine and hot flashes:** in a survey of 1,806 women at the Mayo Clinic, caffeine users rated hot flashes as slightly more bothersome (2.30 vs 2.15). This is an association, not a trial [11]. **Grade: Early.**
- **Alcohol and sleep:** at all doses, alcohol shortens the time to fall asleep but disrupts the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep [12]. **Grade: Moderate** (lab studies in healthy adults).
- **Alcohol and hot flashes:** observational findings are inconsistent and there are no trials [1]. **Grade: Insufficient.**

## B. Supplements

### 6. Magnesium for sleep
A meta-analysis found 3 RCTs in 151 older adults with insomnia [13].
- People taking magnesium fell asleep 17.4 minutes faster than with placebo (95% CI −27.3 to −7.4).
- **Null result:** the 16-minute gain in total sleep time was not significant.
- Risk of bias was moderate to high, and the evidence quality was low to very low.
- I found **no trial in menopausal women**.

**Safety [14].**
- The US/Canadian upper limit for magnesium from **supplements and medicines is 350 mg/day** for adults. There is no limit for magnesium in food.
- The main side effect is diarrhea. Nausea and cramps can also occur. Risk is higher with kidney problems.
- Magnesium reduces absorption of bisphosphonates (bone drugs) and of tetracycline or quinolone antibiotics, so take them at different times.
- The RDA for women 31 and older is 320 mg/day from all sources [14][31c].

**Grade: Limited**, in older adults (not specific to menopause).

### 7. Creatine
**Strength and lean mass with training.** A meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (721 men and women, mean age 57–70, 7–52 weeks) found that adding creatine to resistance training gave more lean mass (+1.37 kg), chest press strength (SMD 0.35) and leg press strength (SMD 0.24) [15].
- **Grade: Moderate**, in older adults of both sexes, alongside training.

**Bone.** A 2-year RCT gave 237 postmenopausal women (mean age 59) creatine (0.14 g/kg/day) or placebo during resistance training and walking [16].
- **Null result:** no effect on bone density at the hip, femoral neck or spine.
- Creatine helped keep some measures of hip bone geometry and slightly improved walking speed.
- **Null result:** no extra gain in strength.
- **Grade: Insufficient** for bone density, in postmenopausal women.

**Memory.** A meta-analysis of 8 RCTs in healthy people found a small benefit (SMD 0.29), with high heterogeneity [17].
- The benefit was larger in adults aged 66–76 (SMD 0.88) and absent in younger people.
- No trials were in menopausal women.
- **Grade: Limited**, in general adults.

### 8. Vitamin D and calcium
**Intakes for women 51 and older [31a, 31b; consistent with the IOM report, 19].**
- Vitamin D: 600 IU (15 mcg) a day to age 70, then 800 IU (20 mcg). Upper limit 4,000 IU.
- Calcium: 1,200 mg a day. About 2,000 mg a day from all sources is considered safe after age 50.

**VITAL trial [18].** 25,871 US adults (women aged 55 and older) took 2,000 IU of D3 a day or placebo for a median of 5.3 years. Participants were not selected for deficiency.
- **Null result:** no effect on total, non-spine or hip fractures (total HR 0.98).
- This held regardless of starting vitamin D level.
- **Grade: Insufficient** for fracture benefit in non-deficient adults.

**Testing.** The Endocrine Society advises against routine vitamin D blood tests without a specific medical reason. It also advises against doses above the standard intakes for healthy adults under 75 [20].

### 9. Herbal and other supplements for symptoms
The overall verdict in [1]: supplements and herbal remedies are **not recommended** for hot flashes (Levels I–II).

**Black cohosh.**
- Cochrane review (16 RCTs, 2,027 peri- and postmenopausal women, median 40 mg a day for about 23 weeks): **no difference from placebo in hot flash frequency** [21].
- [1]: Level I, not recommended.
- A 2023 meta-analysis (22 trials, 2,310 women, including combination products) found small benefits for overall symptoms (g 0.58) and hot flashes (g 0.32), but none for anxiety or depression [22].
- NCCIH calls the results "inconsistent" [30a].
- **Grade: Insufficient** (mixed; the most rigorous review found no effect).

**Soy isoflavones.**
- [1]: mixed evidence. Products, doses and trial lengths vary a lot. Soy foods, extracts and equol are Level II, not recommended. Soy is a common allergen.
- Cochrane review (43 RCTs, 4,364 women): "no conclusive evidence" that phytoestrogens reduce hot flashes. Hot flashes fell 1–59% with placebo. High-genistein extracts (>30 mg a day) looked promising. There was no harm to the uterine lining over up to 2 years [23].
- **Grade: Limited.**

**Red clover.**
- Cochrane pooled 5 trials of one extract and found **no significant difference** (−0.93 hot flashes a day, CI −1.95 to 0.10) [23].
- A 2021 meta-analysis of 8 trials found −1.73 a day (CI −3.28 to −0.18), with high heterogeneity [24].
- [1] does not discuss red clover by name.
- **Grade: Limited.**

**Evening primrose oil.**
- The only trial (56 women, 500 mg a day for 6 months) showed no benefit. Hot flashes fell 1.0 a day with the oil vs 2.6 with placebo [1].
- **Grade: Insufficient.**

**Sage.**
- Not discussed in [1].
- A meta-analysis of 4 small trials (310 postmenopausal women) found a pooled effect on frequency of ES −1.12 (95% CI −2.37 to 0.14). That interval **crosses zero**, even though the authors call it significant. Severity did not change significantly [25].
- **Grade: Early.**

**Ashwagandha.**
- Not discussed in [1].
- RCT in 100 perimenopausal women (300 mg twice a day vs placebo, 8 weeks): menopause symptom and quality-of-life scores improved, and hormone levels shifted (estradiol up, FSH and LH down) [26].
- A second RCT (60 women aged 45–55, 56 days) reported similar results [27].
- Both trials were small, short and done in India.
- NCCIH lists menopause as "not enough evidence" [30b].
- **Safety (NCCIH):** rare liver injury. Avoid in pregnancy, while breastfeeding, and with thyroid or autoimmune disease. It may interact with medicines for diabetes, blood pressure and thyroid, and with sedatives and immunosuppressants.
- **Grade: Limited.**

**Omega-3.**
- One 8-week trial (n = 91) was positive. The larger MsFLASH trial (355 women, 12 weeks) found **no difference** from placebo [1].
- **Grade: Insufficient.**

**Also not recommended in [1]:** dong quai (it may interact with warfarin), ginseng, maca, wild yam creams (some contained undisclosed steroids), vitamin E and pollen extract.

### 10. Iron
- In the SWAN cohort (1,320 US midlife women), **34.5%** had three or more episodes of 3+ days of heavy bleeding during the transition [28].
- The WHO (2023) recognizes heavy menstrual bleeding as a risk factor for iron-deficiency anemia [29].
- The iron RDA is 18 mg/day for women 19–50 and 8 mg/day from age 51 [31d].
- Women should have their iron checked before taking a supplement. Heavy or long periods are a reason to see a doctor in their own right.
- I could not open the ODS iron fact sheet, so no upper limit is given here.

### 11. Safety cautions
- **Black cohosh and the liver.** NCCIH reports rare cases of liver damage, some very serious. It is unclear whether black cohosh caused them, and some products contained the wrong herb. Warning signs include dark urine and fatigue [30a]. US Pharmacopeia reviewers found 30 possibly related reports and asked for a label warning: stop use and see a provider if you have liver disease or signs of liver trouble (abdominal pain, dark urine, jaundice) [1].
- **Interactions.** Supplements can interact with medicines, and little is known about their long-term safety [30c]. St. John's wort makes many medicines less effective. Some supplements raise bleeding risk or affect anesthesia [30d]. Magnesium affects absorption of bone drugs and some antibiotics [14].
- **Hormone therapy.** I found no opened source on specific supplement–HRT interactions. NCCIH says phytoestrogen supplements "may not be safe for women who shouldn't take estrogen" [30c].
- **Tell your doctor.** NCCIH advises telling all your health care providers about any supplements you use [30c].

## (b) Safe things to say (email wording)

1. "CBT for insomnia has been tested in women in perimenopause and after menopause. In one trial, most women no longer scored as having insomnia six months later."
2. "The Menopause Society lists CBT and clinical hypnosis among the non-hormone options with good evidence for bothersome hot flashes."
3. "Fans, layers and a cooler bedroom are easy comfort steps. They just haven't been well tested as a treatment."
4. "Strength training builds muscle at any age. In one supervised trial, postmenopausal women with low bone density improved their spine bone density over 8 months."
5. "Caffeine can cut your sleep. One review suggests having your last coffee about nine hours before bed."
6. "Alcohol may help you nod off, but it tends to break up sleep later in the night."
7. "Studies of magnesium and sleep are small and low quality. If you try it, keep supplements to 350 mg a day or less unless your doctor advises otherwise."
8. "In a large trial, vitamin D did not lower fracture risk in people who weren't low in it. The daily basics for women over 50 are 600–800 IU of vitamin D and 1,200 mg of calcium, counting food and supplements."
9. "Research on herbal remedies like black cohosh, soy and red clover for hot flashes is mixed. The Menopause Society does not recommend them for hot flashes."
10. "Heavy or long periods are common in perimenopause. Raise them with your doctor, who can check your iron before you take any."

Always add: "Talk with your doctor or pharmacist before starting a supplement, especially if you take medicines or hormone therapy."
